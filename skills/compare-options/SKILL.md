# Compare real options

The options already exist. The work is finding which of their differences are
decision-relevant, and saying which way those point.

**First, check this is a decision and not a question.** "Postgres or SQLite for
a side project" and "which framework is faster" are answered by facts about the
things themselves — anyone asking gets the same answer, and the right reply is
the technical one. A decision is the other kind: the answer depends on who is
asking, what they already have, and what they would be giving up. If the
situation isn't doing any work in the answer, this skill has nothing to add —
answer the question directly and leave the frame alone.

## Work through it

1. **Restate the options** as they really are, including any the user is
   treating as unavailable but isn't — and including "neither, for now" when
   that is a live choice.
2. **Find the factors that could flip the answer.** Two or four, not ten. If a
   factor comes out the same for every option, it is not part of the decision,
   however important it is in life.
3. **Say which way each factor points, and how strongly.** "B is better on
   money, and it isn't close" is worth more than a table.
4. **Separate reversible from irreversible.** An option you can walk back from
   in a month is worth more than its scorecard suggests; one that closes doors
   is worth less.
5. **Name the opportunity cost.** What choosing one actually gives up, in
   concrete terms rather than "flexibility".
6. **Look for lopsided downside.** Where the worst case of one option is much
   worse than the other's, that usually dominates everything else — even when
   it is less likely.
7. **Say what you would need to know to be sure**, and whether it is cheap
   enough to go find out.

## Then answer

Give a view. "They're both reasonable, it depends on what you value" is a
non-answer; the user can produce that themselves. Say which one the factors
favour, under what conditions that flips, and what would change your mind.

If they are genuinely close, say that plainly and say what that means: when two
options are this close, the cost of deciding slowly usually exceeds the
difference between them.

## No invented arithmetic

Don't assign scores, weights or probabilities just to make the comparison look
quantitative. A weighted table built from numbers nobody measured is a way of
hiding a judgement, not making one.

Use numbers only when the user asks for them, or when the inputs are real —
actual salaries, actual runway, actual hours. Then show the arithmetic plainly
and say what it assumes.

Keep the weight proportionate to the stakes, and remember what the output is
for: if the user finishes reading and still can't act, the comparison failed.
