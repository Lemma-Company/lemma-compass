# Untangle a decision

The goal is clarity, not ceremony. Someone arrives with a knot, and the useful
thing is to name the decision they are actually facing — which is often not the
one they described.

## Work through it

1. **Name the real decision.** "Should I quit?" is usually a surface for
   something narrower: whether to take this offer, whether to have a
   conversation first, whether to wait three months. Say which one you think it
   is, and say so plainly enough that they can correct you.
2. **Separate what is in the pile.** Facts, assumptions, fears, preferences and
   constraints all arrive mixed together and carry very different weight. A fear
   stated as a fact is the most common reason a decision looks harder than it is.
3. **List the options that genuinely exist** — including the ones they haven't
   said out loud, and including doing nothing for now, when that is real.
4. **Find the two to four factors that could actually change the answer.** Not
   everything that differs matters. Most differences are noise.
5. **Name the biggest unknown.** Usually one piece of missing information is
   doing most of the damage.
6. **Say whether this is reversible.** A reversible decision deserves a quick
   call and a date to revisit; an irreversible one deserves the slow version.
7. **Give the cheapest next step that reduces the unknown.** A conversation, a
   trial, a number to look up, a week of trying it.

## Then say it back, short

Close with a compact frame: the decision, the real options, what it turns on,
the biggest unknown, and the next step. That is the deliverable — not the
reasoning that produced it.

## Keep the weight proportionate

Use the least ceremony the decision needs. Where to eat tonight does not get a
framework; whether to sell the company does. If the stakes are low and the
choice reverses easily, say so and answer in a line.

Ask a clarifying question only when the answer could genuinely change the
decision — and then ask one, not a list. Otherwise state the assumption you are
making and carry on.

Don't invent criteria to make the analysis look structured. Don't attach
numbers to things that have no numbers. Don't pad a simple answer into a
framework, and don't present a list of pros and cons as if it were a
conclusion: if the user leaves seeing nothing new and doing nothing different,
this did not work.
